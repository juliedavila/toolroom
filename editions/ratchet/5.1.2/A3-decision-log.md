# Annex A3: Decision log

*Ratchet: closed-loop security validation for AI-assisted engineering. Annex. Edition 5.1.2, Thesis 1.4.2, 2026-10-30, build 13fabeae+local.*

*The decisions that shaped the design, in order, each with its reason and the alternatives weighed.*

| # | Decision | Why | Alternatives weighed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The design lives in one checked model, and every diagram and table is generated from it. | Hand-drawn diagrams disagreed with each other; a model can be checked. | Keep the research's diagrams; maintain diagrams by hand. |
| 2 | Ownership questions are modeled as scenarios, never answered for one organization. | Who the checking team reports to varies by organization; the scenarios showed ownership changes coordination cost, not the plan's structure. | Pick one ownership model; leave ownership unaddressed. |
| 3 | Incident and near-miss reviews ask "what would have caught this first", by hand at first, automated later. | Real intrusions are the loop's best source of missing scenarios. | Treat incidents only as notification clocks. |
| 4 | Regression cadence climbs a ladder (periodic, scheduled, continuous) through a trust gate and steps down automatically. | Cadence depends on the organization; standing permission has to be earned. | Fixed cadence; continuous from the start. |
| 5 | Work is cut into small units that ship continuously; phases mark evidence, never releases. | Release-shaped plans turn into stage gates; units give the tightest feedback. | Release rows as phases; a numbered roadmap. |
| 6 | The design is a generic reference any organization fits through a profile; technology examples are footnotes, never requirements. | It has to serve organizations with different stacks, regulators and appetites. | A design for one organization; a vendor-specific design. |
| 7 | A regression gate's outage behavior is a risk decision set in advance from consequence, never from a model's view of exploitability. | Blocking every deploy is costly, and exploitability scores are a weak signal. | Always fail closed; always fail open. |
| 8 | The asset owner is the line-of-business leader, who confirms tiers the testing team drafts; risk ownership is two roles, business and security. | Owners know consequence; drafting from existing material keeps their effort low; residual risk belongs with the business. | One risk owner; owners draft their own tiers. |
| 9 | Repeated weaknesses become paved-road entries, verified independently and kept under their own regression test. | The road is the loop's second, compounding output. | Fix each finding service by service. |
| 10 | Existing security tools become labeled leads, never facts or coverage, and each tool faces a measured keep, move or retire decision. | Their output is imperfect signal; agents can use it to find real paths. | Ignore existing tools; count their findings as coverage. |
| 11 | Build or buy is decided per part, case by case; the parts that enforce the rules stay under the organization's control whatever is bought. | Appetite for building varies, and large organizations often can build. | One build-or-buy stance for the whole design. |
| 12 | Path availability is stated as worked out, with no spending on redundancy to reach targets on paths that fail safe. | An outage on those paths cannot cause an unapproved effect; it delays fixes, which queue age shows, and the deploy gate, which blocks other teams, meets its target. | Add redundancy until every path meets its target. |
| 13 | A public cloud overlay maps every part to candidate services, starting with AWS, as a profile choice outside the core. | Most organizations build on a cloud; the trade-offs that keep the rules in the organization's hands differ by provider. | Cloud-neutral only; a cloud-specific core. |
| 14 | Typed decision models enter as advisory routes, qualified per decision type on the organization's own sampled findings, and a model check keeps their answers out of every decision. | They fit fast sorting and ranking decisions; calibration has to be earned locally. | Exclude them; let them gate decisions. |
| 15 | Every safety rule is a should-never: exceptions are recorded, expiring decisions by the business risk owner with the security risk owner's concurrence, informed by a trade-off card for each role. | Organizations make legitimately different risk decisions; absolute rules push those decisions out of sight. | Absolute rules; only the four core rules absolute; everything adjustable, including consistency checks. |
| 16 | Adoption is progressive: each profile section belongs to the first phase that needs it. | No organization knows every answer at the start. | Require a complete profile before anything runs. |
| 17 | Markdown is the source of truth; every other form is generated from it. | It is the easiest form to iterate on together and to carry between environments. | HTML as the source; separate documents per audience. |
| 18 | A recorded break from a should-never rule is called an exception. | Every security organization already runs an exception process with the same fields, so the record can go through it. | Departure; waiver, which sounds permanent; risk acceptance, already the name of a ship-through override. |
| 19 | Before the risk owners are named, the program decision owner accepts phase 1 only exceptions, with the independent reviewer concurring; they end with phase 1 and cover only rules whose effects stay in the test environment. | Keeps phase 1 at three working roles while keeping two signatures, one of them independent. | Name both risk owners before phase 1, which is slow and political for a synthetic-target experiment. |
| 20 | Phase 1 stays outside the model-injection rule only while the independent reviewer confirms its boundary: a team-written target, no outsider text, the model route as the only exit, and a synthetic context. A false condition is refused under that rule. | One missing leg was fragile and unchecked; two missing legs, confirmed, hold. | Rely on the declaration alone; require the request-data gate in phase 1. |
| 21 | What counts as a team, and where internal audit sits, are recorded profile choices with stated considerations and no default; the checker only warns when audit takes an operating role. | Both depend on each organization's structure, control framework and audit mandate. | A default definition of team; internal audit as the verifier by default. |
| 22 | Phase 2 starts only when the decision owner accepts the phase 1 report against criteria written in advance, including each role's account of departures from plan; approvers see the outcome of every run they approved; the hazard analysis of Ratchet's own control structure runs after that report; each service owner sets its delivery tolerance. | An editorial review of the six-pager found the approval bundled evidence-gathering with spending, the approvers had no feedback, and the hazard analysis would have studied the loop on paper instead of the loop people ran. | Approve both phases as one block; measure approvers without telling them outcomes. |
| 23 | The five checkpoints are renamed phases, each reporting green, yellow or red, and phases are planned and judged in weeks or days, never tracked in hours; where a cost needs effort, it is estimated, for example from role-based rates. A phase over its planned weeks turns yellow; only a stop condition, such as phase 1 reaching the end of its six-week timebox, turns it red. | Leaders read progress as phases with a status; hours tracking is toil nobody will keep up, and slips that matter show in days or weeks. Stopping phase 1 for running long would end the measurement it exists to make. | Keep checkpoints; track hours per role; stop phase 1 on an hours overrun. |
| 24 | "Answer key" is renamed "proof condition": what the running system must show for a weakness to count as real, and what would show it is not. The rule now keeps it from the testing agent, and a language model may draft it for the scenario owner on a separate route with no tools. | Readers did not know the old term; findings from discovery agents are valid locally and unproven end to end, so the condition has to define end-to-end proof; owners will realistically write these with a model. | Keep "answer key"; "expected result"; forbid any model from drafting it. |
| 25 | Where phase 1's one weakness comes from is the organization's choice: a practice target, a finding from its existing discovery practice, or a recent finding. With a practice target the four synthetic conditions hold; with a real finding the agent drafts only, holding no tools, its one exit the approved model route, and people run every approved action. | Organizations start from different places, and a real finding shows the real handoffs that a synthetic case cannot. Outsider text and real data cannot be kept from an agent working on a real finding, so the safeguard moves to taking away the agent's means to act. | Practice target only; real findings with the agent holding tools. |
| 26 | Ratchet starts by mapping how the loop works today (an optional current-practice section, filled with the people who run each step), and the organization's existing discovery practice enters as a lead source. A scenario owner turns a lead into a scenario; a tool-less model may draft the proof condition, shown only to that owner, and a model check confirms nothing it writes reaches a test-drafting agent. | Most organizations already run discovery, often people pointing agents at repositories or infrastructure, at uneven maturity; the plan has to start from that position. Proof conditions will realistically be drafted with a model, so the drafter is isolated by construction rather than by policy. | Assume no loop exists; keep discovery outside Ratchet; let the test-drafting agent draft its own proof condition. |
| 27 | The thesis names earned autonomy as the direction for learning. Ratchet starts by learning only from what people confirm. The owners of business and security risk can grant it the right to learn from its own validations, one kind of judgment at a time, as the one standing departure from the rules on validation and independent acceptance (written, expiring, and with every self-validated finding labeled so measures read with and without them), on two records checked on samples it cannot predict by a different means: honesty (what it reported matches what the control plane recorded) and correctness (its verdicts hold up). One such means is a deterministic replay: the agent hands over its reproduction as exact steps, a runner with no model replays them within the approved scope, and the outcome is read from what the control plane observed and judged against the proof condition the agent never saw. The right lapses when either record slips. The part that proposes a test never judges it, and authority to act never moves this way. | Confirmation by people does not scale with agent volume. A named grantor keeps this inside the departure discipline; honesty alone shows reporting is truthful, not that a verdict is right, so correctness is measured too. The design already moves this way: phase 3 automates the verifier, and model routes earn each decision type on measured reliability and are narrowed when it breaks. | Learning only from people, permanently; autonomy scoped to learning and priorities only, never validation; autonomy earned automatically with no grantor. |
| 28 | Ratchet meets Jig, the secure-creation loop where developers and coding agents write code, at declared interface points. Proven failure classes, road entries with a current assurance record and derived checks go out; provenance per change and leads from each layer come back. Only static and in-process checks that report pass or fail reach a system where coding agents run, and a model check confirms no proof condition, expected-outcome hash or outsider text reaches it. Provenance is a declared fact, and unlabeled changes stay in every denominator. | Each loop can run alone, so they share rules and meet only at named points, and each can change its internals. Coding agents inside Jig would read anything sent there, so rule 2 and the quarantine have to hold at that boundary. Evaluation, independent of Jig and of the road owner, assigns the layer that should have caught each accepted defect, and Jig supplies only where it did catch it, so Jig never grades its own layers. | Merge the two designs; send Jig every regression, including runtime replays; let Jig count its own catches. |
| 29 | Ratchet is a core platform (the loop, its four rules and the record) with pluggable modules. Each module declares, in one place, its category (signals in, stakes, reach or prevention out), its interface points with what crosses and in which contract, its switch-on test, how it keeps each of the four rules, the separations it needs and the existing practice it wires in. Secure creation, the loop where developers and coding agents write code, becomes the fifth module, beside the paved road, and depends on it for the shared measures. Each interface point climbs a wiring ladder (connected, conformant, aligned) with its target declared from day one; the organization records where each point stands, and the profile check warns below conformant and refuses only a connected point that reaches a part coding agents can reach. | Most organizations already run some version of several modules, disconnected from each other, so adopting a module is mainly wiring an existing practice in, and the interface comes first because existing systems are rarely to standard. A tag on parts said nothing about where a module plugs in or how it keeps the rules. | Keep modules as tags only; build each module as new parts; refuse every point below conformant; make secure creation part of the core, or keep it outside the module set. |
| 30 | Every relationship that crosses planes carries a contract or records the one-line reason it needs none, and the model check refuses any that does neither. What crosses to the secure-creation loop carries no verdict or fixture-set reference, and the model check refuses those contracts on any route into a part coding agents can reach. An automated verifier or evaluator starts in phase 3 only after the evaluation team is named and differs from the teams it is separated from, refused by default like any other rule. The three secure-creation separations become profile roles with rules of their own; a module interface point below aligned warns, and is refused once it outlasts a limit the organization sets; and earned autonomy runs on a grant record, with every self-validated finding labeled. | A contract per pair of planes let 28 relationships cross with nothing defining what they carried. The separations and verdict boundaries the interface review found were declared and not enforced. Eleven orderings let a verifier or evaluator run as a system before anyone checked who owned it. | Keep the plane-join check per pair; add unit prerequisites for the evaluator separations; block phase 3 readiness without refusing; set a default limit for points below aligned. |
| 31 | An organization can add parts of its own and record changes to shipped ones. A design change that adds a part also gives that part's design-layer entries: its plane, how it is realized, its tier and failure mode, and its cloud placement. The patch tool then compiles the organization's design layer next to its model, and the model checks judge that compiled layer. Every organization part therefore has a plane, so the rule that every relationship crossing planes carries a contract covers organization parts too. A part with no plane no longer escapes that rule, and the impact report flags an uncontracted crossing before anyone accepts the change. | Ratchet ships mechanisms, not permutations: the organization states its own specifics and the same checks judge them, so the authors never have to anticipate every setup. A trial adoption found that adding any part failed the design-layer coverage checks in every edition, and no design change could satisfy them. The adopter folded its own service into a shipped part and lost that service's owner and tier. The same trial showed the plane rule skipped parts with no plane, which is exactly the joins an organization adds. | Exempt organization parts from coverage (rejected: an organization part would then have no tier, failure mode or plane, and the plane rule would stay blind); keep a hand-maintained organization copy of each layer file (rejected: nothing would keep the copies in step with the patches that justify them); require layers on every add as a hard patch error (rejected for this edition: a design change that passed the patch tool before would then fail it; the model checks already refuse the part until its entries exist). |
| 32 | Three rules for an organization's own changes. An error one design change adds counts against it only if no later change built on it removes that error, so fixing a change with a follow-up change works. Any change to where a part runs, after the organization adopts its own placement file once, is a design change like any other, so it gets an impact report, an accepter and a version. A risk report that differs from its recorded copy only because a new edition laid the report out differently is restamped, never sent back for acceptance. | A second trial adoption followed the edition's own advice and fixed a change with a follow-up change, and every upgrade then flagged the original. The same trial moved a control part from a managed cloud account to a self-run cluster without any review, because placement sat outside the design changes. It also had to re-accept every accepted change after an upgrade that renamed nothing. Each rule keeps review where meaning changes and removes it where nothing did. | Judge each change alone and fix by replacing and withdrawing (rejected: the history churns and the workflow's own advice fails); a separate sign-off rule for placement (rejected: no impact report, and a second path to keep in step); re-accept after any report change (rejected: review with nothing to review trains people to approve unread). |
| 33 | A kept regression test leaves the blocking path only by a recorded route. It retires when a kept, passing check that costs less covers its weakness class, that check fails against the test's reverted fix, and the security risk owner accepts. When it has not failed for a period the organization sets, only its extra time-based runs move to a scheduled lane; it still runs, and still blocks, on every change that touches its declared dependencies. A flaky one is quarantined as a capped exception. Each is re-proved on a schedule against its fix reverted, and a "no longer fails" result moves it out of the blocking lane only after the verifier confirms it. The thesis is stated as fewer security escapes per deployment and a shorter time to remediate, measured beside DORA's delivery metrics, and a purple team role turns findings into detections and kept tests under the same four rules. | Each closed finding adds a kept test, so a suite that only grows would eventually tax the delivery speed the design promises to protect. Delivery practice already answers this: keep feedback fast, run only what a change can break, quarantine flaky tests with limits, prune what no longer earns its place, move checks to cheaper levels, and check that a test can still fail. The organization sets every number, because the right periods and caps depend on its pipeline. | Never retire, only demote (rejected: the scheduled lane grows without bound); retire on quiet alone (rejected: a weakness that returns after the period escapes); rely on the trust gate for flaky tests (rejected: nothing makes anyone fix the test); count catches only (rejected: it cannot tell a quiet working test from a broken one). |
| 34 | Each escape has an operational definition and carries its cost. The profile declares where escapes are detected and by which role, the window after a deployment within which an escape counts toward that deployment, and a confirming role apart from every detecting role, the producer team and the program decision owner; the checker refuses a confirmer who is any of them, and an escape left unconfirmed past an optional alert age is raised to the security risk owner. Each record names who attributed the escape to a deployment and how; an escape no deployment shipped is reported in its own bucket, and the rate is shown with late escapes included as well as without. Each escape's record holds its blast radius (the consequence tiers of the assets reached and the services affected), its time to recover, and whether the fix removed the weakness class or one instance, and from phase 2 on the report shows those consequences beside the counts. Phase 1 deploys the weakness it just fixed back into a test environment, by a route the kept regression test does not guard, to time how monitoring or scheduled discovery detects it and how the loop recovers, when the service owner estimates that costs a day or less; otherwise the experiment runs in phase 2. | A count treats every escape alike, so two programs with the same escape rate can carry very different risk, and a count with no operational definition lets whoever detects an escape decide alone whether it counts. The cost fields reuse facts the design already holds (consequence tiers, closure states and deployment records), so they add no new attestation. Deploying a fixed weakness again tests what watches the running system, which the replay stage and the deliberate reintroduction (both pipeline gates) leave untested, and the one-day bound keeps it from taxing the service owner in phase 1. | Model and measures only, with no profile field (rejected: each organization would define detection sources, window and confirmer in its own notes, nothing would check the confirmer against the detectors, and escape rates would mean different things in different organizations); make the section required (rejected: every existing profile would fail, which makes the release a major; phase 2 readiness requires it instead). |
| 35 | Trust grows inside limits set by design. The design caps what each agent may touch: for each agent route and each kind of judgment, a limit names the highest consequence tier, the environments and the action classes, and the role that set it, and the organization sets a consequence line at or above which a person approves every action. People approve any action outside a limit; inside it, a reviewed test may run on its grant alone once the trust gate of clean runs holds, always through the authorization service the agent cannot reach. Standing permissions, earned-autonomy grants and dials all sit under the limit, and the record widens an agent's scope only within it; a profile with a grant wider than its limit is refused unless it records an exception. Acceptance gates keep people. Phase 2 records, before every agent action, its route's recent accuracy and how far its limit reaches, measures each wrong action's cost apart from both, and reports which better forecast it. The security risk owner sets each limit and the business risk owner sets the consequence line. With no limits declared, approval works as it did before limits existed: kept regression tests replay on their standing permissions, and every other action is approved by a person. | At agent volume a per-action approval turns into a rubber stamp, and a long run of correct calls says nothing about how much damage the next wrong one can do. Authority gates and acceptance gates answer different questions: whether an action may happen, and whether a claim is true. Only the first can safely tier by consequence. | Trust that grows with the agent's accuracy alone, rejected because accuracy does not bound the cost of an error and attackers adapt to what the record rewards. A readiness gate in place of a refusal, rejected because a missing answer only blocks a phase while a grant past its limit is an active widening of authority. Keeping a person on every action, rejected as the long-run design because approval quality decays with volume; it stays the behavior until limits are declared. |
| 36 | Security pays for the fix path. Before a validated finding enters an owning team's queue, the security side supplies a test that reproduces it, a drafted fix the team takes, edits or declines, and an owned gate: the regression test the fix must pass, with a named owner. Where no build-level test exists, a reproduction through the running system serves as the test and the gate; where no fix can be drafted, the reason is recorded and the finding goes to the owning team's lead and the security risk owner. Each owning team sets its fix cap with security, which is the same team WIP cap the saturation signal reads. A part in the control zone applies the cap, checking it before the fix path is prepared and again at queue entry. A finding above the cap is logged with its reason, stays accepted on the record, and is reviewed with the team every month, the security role that agreed the cap co-signing each review; a finding at or above the consequence line enters regardless, logged as over the cap, and one held at two consecutive reviews goes to the security risk owner, who sends it or records a risk acceptance with an expiry. The program's sponsor holds both sides to agreed caps and sets the default for any team that sets none, which phase 3 readiness requires. | The authors judge that owning teams discount security's tickets when most are noise, and that a cap that security sets on their intake turns a discounted ticket into a resented one. When the fix path costs a team less than its queue does, choosing it stops being a burden, and a team that sets its own cap has agreed to it. A team that sets no cap would leave its intake open, so the sponsor sets a default. | The sponsor sets every cap (rejected: it is security's authority over another team's intake again, one level up); caps booked centrally by the program (rejected: the same, and nobody on the owning team agreed the number); drop findings above the cap silently or leave them to age in the queue (rejected: either hides the refusal the monthly review exists to see); keep the fix path a hands-on offer with no obligation (rejected: a team could still receive a bare ticket and do the work itself); apply the cap in the connector that prepares fix paths, with reviews signed by the owning team alone (rejected: the record of refusals would be written by the parties it constrains); let the cap hold every finding alike (rejected: a finding at the consequence line could wait indefinitely); a default cap that only warns when missing (rejected: a team with none is uncapped, and the saturation signal reads no cap for it). |
| 37 | Phase 1 is a feasibility test. It passes on checks the sponsor writes before it starts (the loop closed, an independent reviewer verified the fix, the regression test held) and records a per-case verdict of the agent against the scripted test from a scorer the sponsor names in the same criteria. Numeric thresholds, both those that stop the work and those that grant each step of trust, are set from the phase 1 map before phase 2. The escape rate keeps DORA's change fail rate as its frame and is compared with it only once both are collected the same way over enough deployments. | One weakness cannot show that the loop works at scale or set a number worth holding the program to; asking it to invites a verdict on rules chosen afterward. Naming the scorer in the written criteria keeps the comparison honest without another profile field. The DORA frame tells delivery teams what the escape rate is for, and the condition stops a reader from comparing two rates collected differently. | Treat phase 1 as a first measurement of agent value (rejected: too few cases to measure anything); a profile field for the scorer (rejected: the criteria already have an owner and a date); drop the DORA comparison until phase 2 data supports it (rejected: the frame is what makes the measure legible to delivery teams). |
| 38 | Every edition ships a strategy brief beside the six-pager: two pages for an executive sponsor, answer first, built as a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions under assertion headings, with one line on what the program will not do, and ending on the decision asked of each role and the conditions that stop the work. It is generated from the same sources as the six-pager, so its counts and stop conditions cannot drift, and it carries a guide for tailoring it to one organization. | The six-pager argues the thesis and holds the evidence; a sponsor deciding whether to start needs the decision and the reasons on two pages, and needs to be able to repeat them. | A standalone page kept in sync by hand (rejected: it drifts the first time the design moves); a choice-cascade strategy (rejected: an internal capability chooses no market); a press release with questions (rejected: the six-pager already holds the questions). |
| 39 | The thesis does not depend on how many weaknesses an organization has. It names two starting points: a backlog that discovery outruns, and a known backlog near zero after a sweep with a capable model. In both, the way findings get fixed is not repeatable at scale and does not hold. Just-in-time find and fix is the North Star in every starting point: it buys throughput in a backlog organization and keeps the number near zero in a swept one. The design optimizes the time from a weakness's introduction to a verified fix that stays fixed, which the escape rate and time to remediate already measure. A weakness is caught where code is written or at pull request time when possible, and proved through the running system when not. Volume becomes the complication for backlog organizations only. Fix capacity as the binding limit stays a graded bet and no longer carries the thesis, and a new premise, graded none, says a one-time sweep decays. The tailoring guide asks which starting point an organization is in and gains an opening for a swept organization. | In the authors' observation, many organizations have already swept their code with a frontier model and fixed what it found, so an opening built on a growing backlog no longer describes them and invites the reply that the problem is solved. A sweep is a batch: its fixes land as a surge, nothing holds them while the code changes, and each more capable model resets the floor, so the case for a loop holds at any backlog size. Organizations tailor the story, so the tailoring guide has to send each one to the opening that fits its starting point. | Keep the volume-led opening and add only a tailoring branch for swept organizations (rejected: the generic story would still tell a swept organization its problem is a backlog it no longer has, and every tailoring would start by undoing the opening); lead with trust, the doubt each agent finding deserves and the measurement it damages (rejected: trust holds in both starting points, but it explains why findings need checking and leaves out why fixes must land fast and stay fixed, which is the part a swept organization doubts). |
| 40 | Each attack-injection experiment names the detection layer expected to catch its weakness class (the workstation, a pull request, the pipeline, a deployed test environment or production) and records whether that layer caught it, which layer did otherwise, and the time to detection and to recovery. The pre-deploy layers need no notice, never merge or deploy the injected change, and repeat as routine re-proof. A deployed test environment or production is reached only under a notice policy the organization declares in an optional profile section: announced, unannounced, or the recommended trusted agent, one role on the detection side, apart from the team that runs the agents, who is told in advance and can stop the exercise. The section also lists the environments allowed. Without it nothing is injected past the pipeline, and production also needs the production-effects module. Phase 1 uses the cheapest layer that should catch the class, usually a pull request or the pipeline, under the same one-day cost bound. The authorization service enforces the policy without relying on any approver: it refuses an injection outside the policy before anyone is asked, gives each injection grant the restore that undoes it and an expiry at the service owner's time limit, and on the trusted agent's stop revokes the grant and restores the environment within a ceiling the organization sets; production also needs an exposure window. The profile check refuses a trusted agent off the detection side, one who is the team that runs the agents, is not named or cannot stop the exercise, production while the production-effects module is off or with no exposure window, and an environment past deployment with no ceiling. | Each weakness class has a layer that should catch it, and the cheapest layers sit before deployment, where an injection touches no running system and needs no warning. Past deployment an injection reaches the people who watch the running system, so the organization decides how they are told. Security chaos engineering is the frame; Ratchet ships the mechanism and leaves the practice to each organization. The seven checks catch contradictions in the declared policy, so they are consistency checks, which no exception can cover; the safety boundary for production already lives in the production-effects module and in the authorization every injection passes, so no new should-never rule is needed. | One notice rule for all layers (rejected: either pre-deploy injections would wait for a notice that protects no one, or injections past deployment would run with none); unannounced by default (rejected: a first exercise past deployment could set off a real incident response with nobody able to stop it); a should-never rule for the trusted agent (rejected: none of the four rules is at stake, and a recorded exception could then let a contradictory policy stand); send the trusted agent's stop to the side that runs the exercise (rejected: the stop would wait on the party it is meant to override); leave refusal past the pipeline to the approver (rejected: one approval mistake would put an injection into a running system with no policy); gate a phase on the trusted agent's answers (rejected: no phase needs the section, and an unanswered section already allows nothing past the pipeline). |
| 41 | A design change that modifies a part, a relationship or a contract may declare what it replaces: for each field it sets, a short hash of the value it was written against. The patch tool refuses the whole change when any hash differs from the field's current value, naming the field and both hashes; it never warns and applies. The authors' own changes carry it from this edition on, and the changes accepted before it stay as they are. An organization's changes may carry it, and the design-change and upgrade workflows recommend it. | A modify replaces each field it sets whole, so a change written against an older value silently drops whatever a newer edition or an earlier change has put there since. The authors once lost a part's escape duties this way and found it only by reading the compiled model. A hash turns that loss into a refusal at apply time, before anything is dropped, and costs nothing while the value has not moved. | Warn on a mismatch and apply (rejected: the text is dropped all the same, and a warning among many is easy to miss, which is how the earlier loss went unseen); required for an organization's changes too (rejected: every change an organization already holds would stop being valid, which makes a safeguard a breaking change, and the organization is better placed to decide when its held changes need it). |
| 42 | A part's own build-or-buy entry overrides its plane's entry. Once the part's choice is answered, the plane's entry no longer covers that part and passes nothing to it: the product, whether the organization keeps control, and who administers the part come from the part's entry alone. While the part's choice is still open, the plane's entry covers it. | Build or buy is decided part by part, and the schema always said a part entry overrides its plane. The checker judged both entries, so an organization that bought a plane and built one rule-enforcing part in it got findings against a part it builds. Honoring the override removes those findings, and no profile that passed starts failing. | Independent entries, each judged on every part it names (rejected: a plane's choice would keep raising findings against a part the organization decided otherwise, against what the schema says); a field-by-field merge, the part's fields over the plane's (rejected: a part bought from another vendor would inherit the plane's product and its answer on organization control, so the part could pass on an answer nobody gave for it). |
| 43 | Each step of the map of how the loop works today may name the role that confirmed its account, with the date in the step's note. A step with no confirmer has not been confirmed yet. No rule compares the confirmer with anyone; one of the people who run the step is the expected case. | The map records today as the people who run each step describe it, and every phase starts from it, so a reader needs to see whose account each step is and which steps nobody has confirmed.
